I am excited to announce the launch of [Odeai](https://www.odeai.earth/), a startup building short-range weather forecasts (i.e., nowcasting), which I am cofounding with Thibaut Cassard and Paul Bonnet-Eymard. Thibaut has been a data scientist specialized in meteorology for six years, at Plume Labs then AccuWeather; Paul spent five years at Air Liquide designing industrial B2B offerings; and I bring my experience at the intersection of AI, high-performance computing (HPC), and data engineering to the project. We are based in Paris.

Rain in the coming hours decides very concrete operational calls: whether a winegrower can spray without the rain washing the treatment away, when water network operators must secure their lift stations, or whether the organizers of a public event welcoming many people must delay, shorten, or cancel for safety reasons. Current solutions answer these questions poorly. The classic numerical weather prediction pipeline first reconstructs the current state of the atmosphere from observations through data assimilation, then runs a physical forecast model on top of it. Each stage introduces latency (the analysis the forecast starts from is typically a few hours old) and information loss (Gaussian error assumptions and imperfect physical parameterizations discard much of what the observations actually contain).

Odeai takes the opposite path: we start directly from raw observations (satellite imagery, radar) and master the entire chain from real-time data to forecast, combining physics and AI. Our long-term vision is an observation-centric, end-to-end differentiable system: learned observation embeddings, a lightweight differentiable dynamical core (i.e., a simplified solver for the equations of atmospheric motion) to enforce large-scale coherence, and ensemble forecasting as a first-class design goal, all without depending on third-party analyses or reanalyses. This is not wishful thinking: observation-based models such as [MetNet 3](https://arxiv.org/abs/2306.06079) and [OMG-HD](https://arxiv.org/abs/2412.18239) have shown that forecasts produced this way can outperform the analysis itself at short lead times. This suggests that the quality ceiling of current AI weather models is an artefact of the processing pipeline, not of the observations.

Concretely, our first nowcasting model is already live over the continental US and Europe: a rain nowcast with a value every minute for the next three hours, refreshed every ten minutes, built on real-time satellite and radar observations as input data. Next on the roadmap: more variables (lightning, hail, wind, sunshine), progressively longer horizons, and a foundational model trained on more heterogeneous observation sources.

I am grateful to work on this project, which combines both my technical expertise and useful applications, in line with my beliefs. In a changing climate, weather is an increasingly critical issue. Let the adventure begin!
